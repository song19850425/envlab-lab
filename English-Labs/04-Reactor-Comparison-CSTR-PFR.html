REACTOR COMPARE LAB · CSTR vs PFR

Same temperature, same residence time — so why do CSTR and PFR give different conversions?
First-order reaction A→B · Arrhenius kinetics · Exponential temperature effect.

About this lab

A continuous stirred tank reactor (CSTR) mixes its contents completely, so reaction happens everywhere at the outlet concentration, while a plug flow reactor (PFR) keeps fluid elements in order, so concentration falls gradually along the tube. For a positive-order reaction that difference usually gives the PFR higher conversion at the same residence time. This CSTR vs PFR conversion comparison simulates a first-order reaction A to B with Arrhenius kinetics, so the rate constant rises exponentially with temperature. You can adjust temperature, residence time, and activation energy, then compare the two conversion curves and the conversion gap directly. It is built for reaction engineering students who want to see, rather than memorise, why reactor choice matters most when the rate is fast and the residence time is short.

Questions students ask

Why does a PFR usually beat a CSTR at the same residence time? In a CSTR the whole volume sits at the low outlet concentration, so the average rate is lower; in a PFR the early sections still see high concentration and react fast.

What does this continuous stirred tank reactor simulation compare? It runs the same first-order reaction, temperature, and residence time through an ideal CSTR and an ideal PFR, then reports both conversions and their gap.

When does the gap between CSTR and PFR get biggest? At high temperature and short residence time, where the rate is fast and concentration differences dominate.

Does a higher activation energy always help? It makes conversion more temperature-sensitive: gains at high temperature are larger, but losses at low temperature are steeper too.

Red line 1 · Thermal runaway
In an exothermic reaction, a temperature rise makes k grow exponentially, which releases heat even faster — if cooling cannot keep up, the reactor runs away. In this isothermal demo temperature is a set value; in real plants it is a state variable that needs interlocked protection.
Red line 2 · Shorter residence time is not always better
Too short a τ leaves the reaction unfinished, giving a falsely low conversion and off-spec product; too long a τ inflates reactor volume for nothing, wasting capital and footprint. Design should find the knee of the kinetics curve.
Red line 3 · Where you measure temperature
A CSTR is well mixed, so one measurement point is enough; in a PFR the temperature varies along the tube, so measuring only at the outlet means missing a hot spot inside. Multipoint temperature measurement is the safety baseline for a PFR.
▸ Model and equations (deterministic demo, no random numbers)
First-order reaction A → B, isothermal: k = A·exp(−Ea/(R·T)) A = 1×10⁸ h⁻¹, Ea = 40/60/80 kJ/mol adjustable, R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) CSTR (well-mixed steady state): X = τk / (1 + τk) PFR (plug flow): X = 1 − exp(−τk) Residence time τ = V/v (reactor volume / volumetric flow rate) For a first-order reaction, always X(PFR) ≥ X(CSTR), and ΔX shrinks as τk grows; Temperature effect: a 10 °C rise in T multiplies k by about e^(Ea·10/(R·T²)) — the larger the activation energy, the more temperature-sensitive. This page is a deterministic demo, consistent with reaction-engineering textbook equations.
