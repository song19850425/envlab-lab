About this lab

Demo: The Darcy friction factor links pressure drop to flow in a straight pipe. In this interactive lab, water at 25 °C flows through a horizontal test pipe, and a U-tube manometer reads the frictional pressure drop. You set the flow rate and choose a pipe diameter, then back-calculate the friction factor λ from the Darcy-Weisbach equation, Δpf = λ(L/d)(ρu²/2), and plot it against the Reynolds number. In laminar flow λ falls as 64/Re, so the log-log line has slope −1. In turbulent flow in a smooth pipe it follows the Blasius relation, 0.3164/Re^0.25, with slope −0.25. Between Re 2000 and 4000 the transition zone is unstable and points scatter. By collecting at least eight points across both regimes, you can test whether measured points fall on the two theory lines, and see why doubling the flow roughly quadruples the pressure drop in turbulent flow.

Red line 1 · Open the valve before starting the pump
Starting a pump with the outlet valve fully closed dead-heads it: the pump overloads and pipe joints can blow off. A centrifugal pump is started with the outlet valve nearly closed, but a reciprocating pump must have its outlet valve fully open (or it will burst).
Red line 2 · Incomplete venting
Air bubbles in the manometer impulse lines make readings read high and fluctuate — vent fully before measuring, and read only once the liquid column is stable.
Red line 3 · Do not force-fit the transition zone
For 2000 < Re < 4000 the friction factor is unstable and scattered data points are normal; do not force them into the laminar or turbulent formula. In engineering, avoid this range where possible.
▸ Model and equations (deterministic demo, no random numbers fitted to results)
Darcy-Weisbach equation: Δpf = λ · (L/d) · (ρu²/2) Re = ρ·u·d/μ Water at 25 °C: ρ = 997 kg/m³, μ = 8.94×10⁻⁴ Pa·s Laminar zone (Re < 2000): λ = 64/Re — slope −1 Turbulent smooth pipe (Re > 4000): λ = 0.3164/Re^0.25 (Blasius) — slope −0.25 Transition zone (2000 ≤ Re ≤ 4000): λ is unstable, approximated as 0.03; scattered data points are normal Manometer: 1 mmHg = 133.3 Pa (mercury column) This page is a deterministic demo: λ is calculated from Re using the equations above, then Δpf is back-calculated — consistent with hand calculation.

The demo model follows the fluid-flow resistance apparatus of chemical engineering principles (a horizontal straight pipe, L = 2 m, water at 25 °C).For teaching demo use only, not as an engineering basis.

Frequently asked questions

What does the friction factor λ tell you?
It condenses wall friction into one number: given λ, pipe length, diameter and velocity, the Darcy-Weisbach equation predicts the frictional pressure drop.

Why does the λ-Re plot use log-log axes?
Because λ spans orders of magnitude and follows power laws — 64/Re in laminar flow and Re^-0.25 in turbulent flow — which appear as straight lines with slopes −1 and −0.25.

What happens if I double the flow rate?
Velocity doubles, so in turbulent flow the pressure drop rises roughly fourfold, since Δpf scales with velocity squared at nearly constant λ.

Why do points scatter between Re 2000 and 4000?
That is the transition zone, where flow is intermittently laminar and turbulent, so λ is unstable and no single formula fits well.
